2.0. Petar Tandara – Ivanov (★ 1734 – ✝ 1817)

Petar Tandara was Ivan’s second son. He died in 1817 at the age of eighty-three, so it is known that he was born in 1734. He was married to Jela, née Budić. They had sons Grgo, Jakov (1773–), Martin, Marijan (1781–), Mijo, Tadija and Marko (1787–), and daughters Manda (1777–) and Jela (–1824). The sons Jakov (1773–), Marijan (1781–), Tadija and Marko (1787–) do not appear in the 1806 census of souls, and Tadija is also absent from the 1819 census. It may therefore be assumed that they were no longer alive at that time or that one of them had moved away from Ričice.

BEGINNING OF MIGRATIONS OUTSIDE RIČICE

In 1819, the family of Martin Tandara, Lovro’s grandfather, lived in Zavelim. Martin’s sons Ivan, Mijo and Grgo are listed in that census. When the Austrian cadastre was compiled in 1835, Martin Tandara, son of the late Petar, was recorded as a property owner in Ričice. At that time, most Ričice clans owned property in Ričice, Zavelim, Podbila and Vir. These villages belonged to the same parish in Ričice. Many families within a single lineage moved from one locality to another, including within Ričice itself. The population was growing rapidly, and new places to live were being sought. In this context, it is understandable that a family, or part of a family, could be recorded in Ričice in one census and in Zavelim in the next, or vice versa. It is known with certainty, however, that Grgo returned from Zavelim to Ričice with his children and continued to live there with his family.

Family member codes: 2.2.3.1.1.3.1-SP-1 Vanja, née Brkljača | 2.2.3.1.1.3.1-K1 Zrinka | 2.2.3.1.1.3.1-K2 Tomislava | 2.2.3.1.1.3.1.1 Zvonimir .

Dr. Bruno Tandara (1937.–1989.) was the most prominent intellectual from Ričice and an important figure in the Croatian political awakening of the late 1960s and early 1970s. As a physician, he earned a reputation for his dedication, expertise and constant availability to people, but he left his deepest mark through his political activity during the Croatian Spring.

He actively participated in cultural and social life, cooperating with Matica Hrvatska and local media. By encouraging public debate, he emphasized the importance of Croatian identity, the development of Dalmatia, a fair distribution of resources within the federation and the protection of national interests. In 1969, he was elected as a representative to the Parliament of the Socialist Republic of Croatia, where he openly advocated decentralization, the strengthening of the Croatian economy, the development of the Imotski region and equality within the then Yugoslav federation.

Because of these views, he became a target of the communist regime. In a politically staged trial in 1972, he was sentenced to several years in prison, which he served at the notorious Stara Gradiška, together with prominent Croatian political dissidents of the time. His sentence and persecution were part of a broader repression of Croatian intellectuals who sought greater national rights and freedoms.

After leaving prison, he returned to the medical profession, remaining a moral pillar of his community. He died in 1989, shortly before the creation of the Croatian state, to which his work made an important contribution. Today he is remembered as a Croatian patriot, intellectual and prisoner, whose work formed part of the foundations of the political awakening that preceded Croatian independence.
