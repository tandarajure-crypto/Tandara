THE THIRD BRANCH OF THE FIRST TANDARA FAMILY

3.0. Ante Tandara – Ivanov, son of Ivan (1730–)

Ante, son of Ivan, married Matija, née Brnas (D.U.). They had six children: sons Ante (1769–1849), Filip (D.U.) and Pavle (1777–), and daughters Jaka (1771–1837), Luca (D.U.) and Ruža (D.U.).

Person codes: Matija née Brnas (3.0-SP-1) | Ante (3.1) | Filip (3.2) | Pavle (3.0-S1) | Jaka (3.0-K1) | Luca (3.0-K2) | Ruža (3.0-K3)

Ante, son of Ivan, was the third son of our first common ancestor, Ivan, who was born around 1695, and was a few years younger than his brother Petar. He had three sons—Ante, Filip and Pavle—and three daughters—Jaka, Luca and Ruža.

Ante, son of Ante, and Filip, son of Ante, continued the family line, while Pavle’s fate is unknown. Apart from his birth record, no further information about him has been found, suggesting that he probably died very young or left Ričice before leaving any further trace in the parish registers.

The eldest son, Ante, moved with his family to Zavelim around 1819, where his branch developed. Filip remained in Ričice, where his line took its clearest shape.

Ante’s line remained firmly connected to Ričice and Zavelim, where it developed most clearly during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. This family nucleus produced the largest number of people bearing the Tandara surname, so Ante’s line is regarded as the most extensive among Ivan’s descendants.

In later generations, members of this branch moved to other parts of Croatia and then to European Union countries, Canada, the United States and Australia, extending the family line far beyond its original homeland.

FIRST SUB-BRANCH OF ANTE ANTIN

3.1. Ante, son of Ante (1769–1849)

As the eldest son of Ante, son of Ivan, Ante married Matija, née Jukić, in 1801. They had three sons—Lovro, Mijo and Petar (1821–)—and six daughters—Pera (1802–), Ruža (1804–), Antica (1807–), Iva (1808–), a second daughter named Antica (1814–), and Marija (1823–). Only the sons Lovro and Mijo continued the family line. Mijo’s descendants acquired the family nickname TUCIĆI, while Lovro’s descendants through his son Marijan became known as LOVRINOVIĆI and those through his son Križan as KRIŽANOVIĆI. According to the 1819 census, Ante lived in Zavelim, while his brother Filip lived in Ričice. Their descendants later spread throughout Croatia and Europe, as well as to the United States and Canada.

They had a son, Martin, who was killed near Bihać in 1942. His third marriage was to Joza Dragun, and they had no children.

Josip Jurin was tragically killed in 1946 in circumstances that have never been officially clarified. According to eyewitness Marijan Budimir (Mijan), who gave his account to Josip’s wife Anica, née Dragun, shortly before his own death, Josip was killed by his next-door neighbour acting at the urging and direction of an older brother. According to that testimony, the motive was material gain: Josip worked as a trader in the cooperative and had access to goods and supplies that were extremely valuable in the post-war period.

In the years after the war, a false account spread publicly that Josip had been killed by retreating “kamišari”. That version was never confirmed in court, and the entire case remained uninvestigated and unprosecuted because of post-war conditions, unsettled social relations and deliberate manipulation of information. The event is presented here solely as family testimony and the historical experience of his descendants, not as a fact established by a court.

Josip’s death and the long uncertainty surrounding its true circumstances profoundly marked the lives of his wife, children and later generations. It was especially difficult to accept that the man who organised the killing later helped Josip’s family in various ways. This created a deep inner struggle for Josip’s son Ivan—my father—who, until his death in 2025, could not bring himself to believe the eyewitness testimony.

Author’s Note

Because of contradictory and manipulative public accounts, our family was subjected for decades to unpleasant labels that did not reflect what we learned from the eyewitness account and from our own family testimony. The claim that Josip had been a victim of the “kamišari” was used by representatives of the authorities at the time as grounds for erecting a monument to him and including his death in official commemorations of the former state. This created the false impression that our family had taken an ideological position. That did not reflect reality: ordinary people at the time had no developed awareness of ideology; heads of households were doing everything they could to feed large families under exceptionally difficult conditions.

I include this note to clearly distinguish official and subsequently shaped folk accounts from the eyewitness testimony and family memory. Its purpose is not to accuse the descendants of the perpetrator today or to create hostility. Relations between our families are now entirely normal and friendly, based on mutual respect and understanding.

That is precisely why this record has value: it represents a historical fact of family memory—an event that was concealed for decades, misinterpreted and absorbed into folk accounts that did not reflect the actual circumstances. It is a memory carried by Josip’s descendants, not as a burden, but as a quiet reminder of the truth preserved by our elders.

Recording this truth does not seek condemnation or revive old divisions. On the contrary, it honours the generation that endured these events and leaves future generations free to live without the burden of the past, but with a clear and honest understanding of what happened. Truth spoken calmly, without bitterness and without any wish to hurt anyone, is the only way to respect the past and ensure a normal, healthy continuation of life for all who come after us.

Sabina Tandara Knezović, listed in the family reference as Sabina Ivičina, is a Croatian political journalist and a prominent reporter for Nova TV. She joined Nova TV's news department in 2005 and covers political affairs, including the Office of the President of the Republic, the Government and parliamentary parties.

She graduated from the Faculty of Political Science at the University of Zagreb and completed a new-media workshop at the University of Missouri through a programme organised by the United States Embassy.

She has reported on presidential, parliamentary and local elections, Croatia's accession to the European Union and official visits by Croatian state leaders. She has also reported from the war zone in Afghanistan, while her public work includes political interviews, reports, live coverage and election programmes.
