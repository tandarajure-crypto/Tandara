Life Across Three Centuries

Introduction

The pages Origin, Genealogy, and Migrations tell us who the Tandaras are, how they are related, and where they went. This page asks how these families lived. The account applies to the region and period from the 18th to the mid-20th century. The Tandaras were part of this community and lived under the same conditions as their neighbours, so this general account applies to them as well. Where family records exist, the general picture is connected to specific people.

Home and Land

Stone was the only abundant material. Traditional building in the region made use of it and reflects a sensible economy of space, materials, and labour, since stone and living conditions shaped the architecture. An ethnological study of traditional architecture in Ričice covers the layout of the village and residential and farm buildings from the 18th and 19th centuries.

Land was scarce and fragmented. The plots belonging to one household did not form a single holding but were scattered throughout the village, as shown by the 1725 land-division record on the Origin page. As a result, each working day involved walking between fields, and tools and livestock had to be moved from one plot to another. The stones had to be cleared before the land could be worked, so the same labour produced both a field and a dry-stone boundary wall that also marked ownership.

Annual Cycle of Work

Life followed the seasons, and none offered a respite.

Spring meant ploughing. Horses or oxen pulled the plough; where it could not pass, people dug with hoes and picks. They sowed maize, barley, and oats, and planted cabbage, chard, onions, and other vegetables in the garden.

Summer brought the harvest and haymaking. Grain was cut with a sickle, while grass and clover were mown with a scythe. Oak branches and ash branches with fresh leaves were also cut. Everything was stacked so the livestock would have enough fodder through the winter. Threshing took place on the village threshing floor, where horses trampled the sheaves and the grain was separated by winnowing in the wind. Sheep, goats, cows, and horses were taken to the summer pastures, so some household members were away seasonally. The pastures were poor, and the low scrub there was difficult to maintain. Goats destroyed it, so their keeping was later prohibited under Yugoslav forestry regulations (Ziani, Šumarski list, 1964).

Autumn was the season for harvesting grapes, figs, walnuts, and other fruit, as well as wild produce such as cornelian cherries and hazelnuts. Food was prepared for winter, and cabbage was fermented.

Winter was the time for slaughtering pigs, guarding food stores, and repairing tools. Even then there was no rest: livestock had to be fed from the summer haystacks, watered, and supplied with water.

Division of Work in the Household

Work was assigned according to strength and age, not personal choice. Men took on the heavier tasks of ploughing, mowing, building, and handling large livestock. Women managed the household, garden, dairy processing, and textile production, and worked alongside men during harvests. From an early age, children looked after livestock, carried water, and helped in the fields. Water from distant springs was carried in large wooden vessels called vučije. Donkeys and horses transported them; where that was not possible, women carried them on their backs. Watermills on the streams ground grain, but depended on water levels, so many stood idle during dry months.

That is why the number of able-bodied household members mattered, and why families were large. Genealogy notes that households often included several generations under one roof. This shows why: each additional pair of hands greatly increased what a household could do, while older people and children also had their own tasks.

Food and Provisions

The diet was simple and mostly plant-based: bread and porridge made from maize, barley, and wheat; milk and cheese; and seasonal vegetables and fruit. Meat was eaten less often, mainly when pigs were slaughtered and on feast days. Hens provided eggs and occasional meat. Salt, lard, and cool underground spaces preserved meat and other provisions, and the goal each year was to make it to the next harvest. Potatoes and tomatoes became more important only during the 19th century.

Risks: Plague, Famine, Influenza, and Accidents

This way of life was highly vulnerable to disruption, and the diseases that swept through the region affected the Tandaras too.

Plague and famine. The chronicle of Župa Biokovska, in neighbouring Zabiokovlje, records a severe famine in 1817. The plague had decimated the working-age population over the preceding four years, leaving the land untilled and livestock neglected. This sequence shows how illness and famine were linked: when the workforce disappeared, there was no harvest.

Spanish flu. At the end of the First World War, in 1918 and 1919, the region was struck by the Spanish flu, known in family memory as “grla.” Many Tandaras died of it. Among them was Pera, née Matišić, the first wife of Jure Križanov (Đulak), who died in 1919. Jure was left without a working helper in the household, and his son without a mother, so he remarried. His second wife also died young: both of his first two wives died early, from the same disease. He married Joza, née Dragun, for a third time. Three marriages by one man show how difficult it was for a household to survive without an adult woman.

Child mortality and accidents. Križan Lovrin (1843–1910) and Pera, née Budimir, had fifteen children, seven of whom died before the age of ten. Their son Ilija was gored by a bull and died in 1875, illustrating the dangers of working with livestock. Luca (1864–1877), daughter of Lovrin, was killed by falling rocks, according to family tradition.

Family Growth and Pressure on the Land

Growth in the number of family members against a fixed amount of land inevitably created pressure. The figures on the Migrations page show this growth: in 1806 there were 20 Tandaras in three families in Ričice; about fifteen years later there were 27 in four families, in Ričice and Zavelim. A one-third increase in fifteen years on the same land meant that each new generation received a smaller share, making departure an economic necessity. This helps explain the larger migrations that followed, first to Zavelim, Tomislavgrad, and Livno, and later to cities and abroad.

Changing Ways of Life

During the 20th century, farming gradually ceased to be the only source of income. Education, paid work, and urban life reduced dependence on the land and on large households. Wars left gaps between generations. Many Tandaras died during the First World War, but we have no information about those who may have died in the fighting itself. That possibility cannot be ruled out and remains open until family members provide evidence. Those killed in the Second World War were Martin Jurin, who died near Bihać in 1943; Križan Ivanov, who died in Zagreb in 1944; and Josip Jurin, who died in 1946 as a consequence of the post-war circumstances. Ante Marijanov was killed in Kusonje in 1991 during the Croatian War of Independence. Today, members of the lineage are doctors, engineers, journalists, and civil servants, while the village has changed from the centre of everyday life into a place of origin and memory.

Conclusion

The history of the Tandara lineage over three centuries is shaped by interconnected causes. Rocky soil and work without machinery required many hands, which meant large households. Large households, along with plague, influenza, and accidents, also meant many child deaths. Population growth on scarce land pushed new generations to leave, and migration, together with education and paid work, changed the family itself. The genealogical dates and migration maps should therefore be read as outcomes of this way of life.

Source Note

This account connects genealogical data with a general description of life in the region. The account of the annual cycle of work and the division of labour applies to the whole community to which the Tandaras belonged. Where an event survives in family tradition, this is stated explicitly: such testimony matters to family memory, but does not carry the same evidentiary weight as an archival record.
