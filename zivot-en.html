Tandara Life Across 3 Centuries

Introduction

The pages Origin, Genealogy, and Migrations tell us who the Tandaras are, how they are related, and where they went. This page asks how these families lived. The account applies to the region and period from the 18th to the mid-20th century. The Tandaras were part of this community and lived under the same conditions as their neighbours, so this general account applies to them as well. Where family records exist, the general picture is connected to specific people.

Stone, Sweat, and the Bread of Our Ancestors. In the karst lands of the Imotski region and neighbouring areas, among bare hills, rocky ground and tiny patches of red soil, everyday survival demanded sustained effort for centuries. Each plot had to be cleared of stone, every loaf earned through labour, and water carried home. Wealth was not measured only in money: a little arable land, grain, livestock, healthy family members and children to carry the family forward were essential. This account evokes those conditions without attributing every described experience to a specific ancestor.

Home and Land

Stone was the only abundant material. Traditional building in the region made use of it and reflects a sensible economy of space, materials, and labour, since stone and living conditions shaped the architecture. An ethnological study of traditional architecture in Ričice covers the layout of the village and residential and farm buildings from the 18th and 19th centuries.

Land was scarce and fragmented. The plots belonging to one household did not form a single holding but were scattered throughout the village, as shown by the 1725 land-division record on the Origin page. As a result, each working day involved walking between fields, and tools and livestock had to be moved from one plot to another. The stones had to be cleared before the land could be worked, so the same labour produced both a field and a dry-stone boundary wall that also marked ownership.

Overcoming the stone. From morning until evening, labourers cleared rocks with picks, hoes, levers and bare hands to gain even a little cultivable soil. Cracked palms, bent backs, worn clothing and modest footwear bore witness to the effort. The removed stones became dry-stone walls and retaining boundaries: the same labour created both the field and its protection. Families cleared land not only for the next harvest but for children and grandchildren. Old stone walls remain silent monuments to their perseverance.

The four photographs connect clearing rocky fields, moving stone and building houses, outbuildings, retaining structures and dry-stone walls. They illustrate traditional ways of working, without establishing that those pictured belonged to the Tandara family.

Annual Cycle of Work

Life followed the seasons, and none offered a respite.

Spring meant ploughing. Horses or oxen pulled the plough; where it could not pass, people dug with hoes and picks. They sowed maize, barley, and oats, and planted raštika (Dalmatian collard greens), cabbage, chard, onions, and other vegetables in the garden.

Summer brought the harvest and haymaking. Grain was cut with a sickle, while grass and clover were mown with a scythe. Oak branches and ash branches with fresh leaves were also cut. Everything was stacked so the livestock would have enough fodder through the winter. Threshing took place on the village threshing floor, where horses trampled the sheaves and the grain was separated by winnowing in the wind. Sheep, goats, cows, and horses were taken to the summer pastures, so some household members were away seasonally. The pastures were poor, and the low scrub there was difficult to maintain. Goats destroyed it, so their keeping was later prohibited under Yugoslav forestry regulations (Ziani, Šumarski list, 1964).

Autumn was the season for harvesting grapes, figs, walnuts, and other fruit, as well as wild produce such as cornelian cherries and hazelnuts. Food was prepared for winter, and cabbage was fermented.

Winter was the time for slaughtering pigs, preparing and curing meat for storage, guarding food supplies, and repairing tools. Even then there was no rest: livestock had to be fed from the summer haystacks, watered, and supplied with water.

Every season brought work and hope. In spring the family waited anxiously for rain to germinate the seed; summer meant long days under a fierce sun and concern that drought might destroy crops and pasture. Autumn harvesting and preserving determined how secure winter would be. When outside work slowed, tools and clothing were repaired by the hearth, stories were exchanged and the next sowing was planned. No season offered complete rest.

Division of Work in the Household

Work was assigned according to strength and age, not personal choice. Men took on the heavier tasks of ploughing, mowing, building, and handling large livestock. Women managed the household, garden, dairy processing, and textile production, and worked alongside men during harvests. From an early age, children looked after livestock, carried water, and helped in the fields. Water from distant springs was carried in large wooden vessels called vučije. Donkeys and horses transported them; where that was not possible, women carried them on their backs. Watermills on the streams ground grain, but depended on water levels, so many stood idle during dry months.

That is why the number of able-bodied household members mattered, and why families were large. Genealogy notes that households often included several generations under one roof. This shows why: each additional pair of hands greatly increased what a household could do, while older people and children also had their own tasks.

Women — the quiet guardians of family life

Women's work did not end upon returning from the fields. There was food to prepare, children and livestock to care for, laundry to wash, wool to process and clothing to sew or mend. Carrying water was especially demanding. The distance to wells, springs or cisterns varied among Tandara hamlets; in some places it was just a few hundred metres, not hours of walking. The main difficulty was the weight.

Water was carried in large wooden vessels known as vučije. When filled, such a vessel could weigh around 30 kilograms. It had to be carried on the back along uneven, stony paths, in heat or cold, and women often performed this work. When water ran out, the journey had to be repeated. Donkeys or horses could carry larger quantities wherever paths allowed. Every drop came at a cost in human effort, and water was carefully conserved.

Childhood and shared labour

Children learned early to tend sheep and goats, collect firewood, help in the fields and care for younger siblings. Schooling was not equally available to all, but daily work passed down practical knowledge of land, animals and seasons. In large households, often spanning several generations, every helping hand mattered, and the family was the foundation of survival.

Food and Provisions

The diet relied on bread and porridge made from maize, barley, and wheat, along with milk, cheese, and seasonal vegetables and fruit. Meat and animal fat also had an important place, especially in households whose members performed demanding physical labour every day.

Fresh meat was not prepared daily. Chicken was among the more readily available kinds of meat, and meat could also be obtained when a family slaughtered or prepared an animal for a wedding, holiday, or other celebration. Part of it might be shared with other villagers, with the understanding that the favour would be returned when those households had a similar occasion. This reciprocity helped families obtain food and maintain ties between households.

Preserved meat was an important and more regularly available part of the diet: bacon, sausages, pršut (dry-cured ham), and other dried or salted products, chiefly from pigs but also from sheep, goats, or cattle. Drying, salting, fat, and cool storage spaces helped preserve food without refrigeration. These meat products, together with bread, porridge, dairy foods, and other provisions, supplied energy and nutrients for strenuous work. Households had to make their stores last until the next harvest. Potatoes and tomatoes became more important only during the 19th century.

Bread worth its weight in gold

In modest stone houses, family life often revolved around the hearth. Bread was baked under a bell-shaped cover or in simple ovens, depending on the household's means. In times of scarcity every handful of flour mattered. When a mother served bread, it represented clearing and cultivating land, harvesting, milling grain, kneading and baking. Children were taught not to waste bread or even its crumbs. This was more than thrift: it was respect for the work of the whole family.

In that world, bread was not merely food but a sign that the family had made it through another day. The photographs of breadmaking and dairy work show more than traditional crafts: these tasks sustained the household.

Risks: Plague, Famine, Influenza, and Accidents

This way of life was highly vulnerable to disruption, and the diseases that swept through the region affected the Tandaras too.

Plague and famine. The chronicle of Župa Biokovska, in neighbouring Zabiokovlje, records a severe famine in 1817. The plague had decimated the working-age population over the preceding four years, leaving the land untilled and livestock neglected. This sequence shows how illness and famine were linked: when the workforce disappeared, there was no harvest.

Spanish flu. At the end of the First World War, in 1918 and 1919, the region was struck by the Spanish flu, known in family memory as “grla.” Many Tandaras died of it. Among them was Pera, née Matišić, the first wife of Jure Križanov (Đulak), who died in 1919. Jure was left without a working helper in the household, and his son without a mother, so he remarried. His second wife also died young: both of his first two wives died early, from the same disease. He married Joza, née Dragun, for a third time. Three marriages by one man show how difficult it was for a household to survive without an adult woman.

Child mortality and accidents. Križan Lovrin (1843. – 1910.) and Pera, née Budimir, had fifteen children, seven of whom died before the age of ten. Their son Ilija was gored by a bull and died in 1875, illustrating the dangers of working with livestock. Luca (1864. – 1877.), daughter of Lovrin, was killed by falling rocks, according to family tradition.

Illness or an accident did more than take a loved one: the loss of a working family member could endanger sowing, harvesting and the household's food supply. Genealogical records of short lives, remarriages and children who died young therefore reflect the struggles of those left behind. Where a cause of death comes from family tradition, it should be understood as valuable memory, not necessarily an archivally confirmed fact.

Family Growth and Pressure on the Land

Growth in the number of family members against a fixed amount of land inevitably created pressure. The figures on the Migrations page show this growth: in 1806 there were 20 Tandaras in three families in Ričice; about fifteen years later there were 27 in four families, in Ričice and Zavelim. A one-third increase in fifteen years on the same land meant that each new generation received a smaller share, making departure an economic necessity. This helps explain the larger migrations that followed, first to Zavelim, Tomislavgrad, and Livno, and later to cities and abroad.

When the stony land could no longer provide enough bread

As inherited plots were divided among descendants, some became too small to support new households. Leaving for other villages, towns and distant countries gradually became a necessity rather than a choice. Those who departed carried their family name, local speech, customs and memories of their ancestors' stone houses. In this way the Tandara surname spread beyond its original homeland.

Changing Ways of Life

During the 20th century, farming gradually ceased to be the only source of income. Education, paid work, and urban life reduced dependence on the land and on large households. Wars left gaps between generations. Many Tandaras died during the First World War, but we have no information about those who may have died in the fighting itself. That possibility cannot be ruled out and remains open until family members provide evidence. Those killed in the Second World War were Martin Jurin, who died near Bihać in 1943; Križan Ivanov, who died in Zagreb in 1944; and Josip Jurin, who died in 1946 as a consequence of the post-war circumstances. Ante Marijanov was killed in Kusonje in 1991 during the Croatian War of Independence. Today, members of the lineage are doctors, engineers, journalists, and civil servants, while the village has changed from the centre of everyday life into a place of origin and memory.

Change did not reach every hamlet at the same time. Machines gradually took over work once done by hand; water formerly carried in vučije came closer to homes and eventually through their taps. Schooling and paid employment transformed everyday life, while the ancestral village became a place of return and remembrance for many descendants. The family's history, however, was shaped not just by economic change but also by wars and personal losses deserving careful documentation.

Conclusion

The history of the Tandara lineage over three centuries is shaped by interconnected causes. Rocky soil and work without machinery required many hands, which meant large households. Large households, along with plague, influenza, and accidents, also meant many child deaths. Population growth on scarce land pushed new generations to leave, and migration, together with education and paid work, changed the family itself. The genealogical dates and migration maps should therefore be read as outcomes of this way of life.

A legacy we must not forget. Old photographs of labourers lifting stones, women carrying water and families baking bread open a window on the world from which generations of our ancestors came. Every boundary wall, overgrown path and abandoned hearth recalls people who worked, raised children and persevered through hardship. Genealogy is therefore more than a list of names, dates and family ties: it seeks to preserve the memory of those who made future generations possible.

Our ancestors could not always leave us wealth, but they left us life, a surname, roots and belonging. Their hands remain in the stone, their footsteps on the old paths, and their names live on through us.

Source Note

This account connects genealogical data with a general description of life in the region. The account of the annual cycle of work and the division of labour applies to the whole community to which the Tandaras belonged. Where an event survives in family tradition, this is stated explicitly: such testimony matters to family memory, but does not carry the same evidentiary weight as an archival record. The origin of the surname, kinship ties, and migration routes are covered on the Origin, Genealogy, and Migrations pages.

The literary descriptions of stone, bread, work and water carrying evoke conditions experienced by the wider community of which the Tandara families were part; they are not, by themselves, documentation that every event occurred to an identified individual.
